# Megastore Anatomy Reference

**Status:** Active research and visual-design reference

**Purpose:** Define the spatial grammar that lets Big Retail grow from a corner
store into a convincing supercenter without prescribing one mandatory floor
plan.

## The useful lesson from Project Hospital

The goal is not to tell the player exactly where every room or aisle belongs.
The game should model enough operational truth that experienced players build
recognizable stores while inexperienced players can create strange but valid
ones, see the consequences, and improve them.

Authenticity should therefore come from **pressures**, not a hidden blueprint:

- customers want short, legible routes and visible destinations;
- stock travels from receiving to storage to the sales floor;
- chilled merchandise needs specialized infrastructure;
- checkouts need queue and exit space;
- employees lose time to long or obstructed replenishment routes;
- departments benefit from useful adjacency, frontage, and cross-selling;
- high-value and service merchandise creates staffing and security needs;
- expansion creates new delivery, parking, utility, and management bottlenecks.

## What the start-screen reference already gets right

The accepted start-screen image is convincing because it uses a strong retail
visual grammar:

- a clear entrance facade, parking, cart storage, and pedestrian approach;
- a front band of registers between the sales floor and the exits;
- long repeated gondola runs with endcaps and generous cross-aisles;
- produce islands with a different fixture language from dry grocery;
- refrigerated cases around the perimeter;
- promotional islands and visual category blocking;
- a clean distinction between customer circulation and merchandise footprint.

It currently reads more like a polished supermarket than a full megastore. That
is not a failure. The missing megastore feeling comes mainly from the absence of
general-merchandise worlds, service departments, and visible back-of-house
organs—not from insufficient decorative detail.

## Recurring supercenter anatomy

Real stores vary, rotate, mirror, and remodel their layouts. The stable pattern
is a set of spatial systems.

### 1. Customer-facing front edge

- one or more entrance/exit vestibules;
- cart storage and pedestrian protection outside;
- a broad checkout/front-end band;
- customer service, returns, pickup, and money/service functions near an exit;
- small leased or specialist services along the front wall where appropriate.

Walmart’s own store-design descriptions place pickup and customer services up
front and treat checkout as several modes rather than one row of identical
registers. Its current store maps likewise expose departments and exact aisle
locations as navigation information.

### 2. Grocery anchor

- produce/fresh food close to a grocery entrance;
- bakery, deli, prepared food, meat, and dairy using perimeter space and work
  rooms;
- a large, regular dry-grocery aisle block;
- frozen and refrigerated cases along walls or in dedicated runs;
- paper, cleaning, pets, baby, and household consumables forming the bridge
  toward general merchandise.

Produce layouts commonly use sight lines and destination items to pull shoppers
through the department rather than merely filling the nearest empty rectangle.

### 3. General-merchandise matrix

- apparel and soft goods often form lower, more open central fixture fields;
- home, housewares, toys, sporting goods, hardware, automotive, and electronics
  form more distinct department worlds;
- health/beauty, pharmacy, vision, and other services cluster where shared
  customer missions or infrastructure make sense;
- seasonal space is flexible and highly visible;
- garden and auto naturally claim exterior edges because they need yards, bays,
  or separate access.

A real 170,000-square-foot prototype plan set separates front and rear grocery,
general-merchandise and grocery vestibules, offices, garden center, auto center,
pharmacy, vision center, online-pickup/fulfillment work, refrigeration, and a
storewide fixture plan. That division is a useful reminder that a megastore is
many operating environments sharing one shell.

### 4. Circulation skeleton

- a broad front action alley;
- one or more front-to-back power aisles;
- major cross-aisles that divide department blocks;
- smaller parallel aisles inside each block;
- endcaps and promotional pads at high-traffic intersections;
- clear paths from entrances to destination departments and from departments to
  checkout.

This skeleton should remain readable at the game camera’s normal zoom. A large
room filled uniformly with gondolas will look like a warehouse of shelves, not a
megastore, even when it contains the correct number of products.

### 5. Back-of-house spine

- truck access and receiving doors away from customer entrances;
- unloading, staging, claims/returns, and waste handling;
- reserve storage connected to replenishment routes;
- dedicated coolers, freezers, food-prep rooms, and utility areas;
- employee offices, break rooms, restrooms, and training space;
- online-order picking, staging, refrigeration, and customer handoff where that
  service exists.

Back-of-house space is essential to the megastore silhouette. Industry research
places the average supercenter near 157,900 square feet, with about 45,000
square feet of grocery sales area, while explicitly noting extensive backroom,
working, and fulfillment areas.

### 6. Site and exterior logistics

- a parking field proportionate to customer volume;
- cart corrals and safe pedestrian crossings;
- curbside pickup stalls and a handoff route;
- delivery drives and loading areas that do not cross the main customer path;
- garden, auto, fuel, or other exterior operations in later phases;
- signage and facade changes that announce major departments from outside.

## Visual growth phases for Big Retail

These are silhouettes, not rigid unlock tiers.

### Phase A — Corner store

- one entrance and one checkout;
- mixed convenience assortment;
- short fixture runs;
- a tiny reserve-stock/receiving area;
- the owner visibly performs most jobs.

### Phase B — Neighborhood market

- a recognizable front end;
- a small grocery aisle block and produce/fresh zone;
- perimeter refrigeration;
- a distinct stockroom and receiving route;
- enough staff that checkout and stocking can occur simultaneously.

### Phase C — Full supermarket

- a serious fresh-food perimeter;
- multiple checkout types;
- wide cross-aisles, endcaps, and seasonal/promotional pads;
- cold storage, food preparation, waste, and larger receiving;
- an initial customer-service or pharmacy-style specialist operation.

### Phase D — Supercenter

- grocery is one large anchor rather than the entire store;
- several visually distinct general-merchandise departments;
- a legible circulation skeleton spanning the whole building;
- multiple services and a larger back-of-house spine;
- parking, pickup, and deliveries create competing site demands.

### Phase E — Regional megastore

- garden, auto, pharmacy/health, fulfillment, foodservice, or other specialist
  operations form stores-within-the-store;
- several entrances, receiving points, storage regimes, and management layers;
- layout changes are driven by throughput and logistics rather than simply by
  access to more floor tiles.

## Design rule

> New scale should require new organs, not merely a larger rectangle.

A department should bring some combination of unique fixtures, stock flow,
staff skills, utilities, service space, security, waste, or customer behavior.
That is what will make each growth phase look and play differently.

## Research sources

- [Walmart Supercenter #2599 store map](https://f.wishabi.net/dynamic_content/data/walmartusa/1016_3/store-2599-marketingmap.pdf) — a real department-level layout showing Food, Apparel, Electronics, Toys, Pharmacy, Sporting Goods, Lawn & Garden, and the front registers.
- [Walmart: A Look Inside Next-Gen Test Stores](https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2017/05/12/a-look-inside-walmarts-next-gen-test-stores) — adjacency clusters, integrated pickup, checkout experiments, and department destinations.
- [Walmart: This Store is Helping Reimagine the Supercenter](https://corporate.walmart.com/news/2016/02/12/this-store-is-helping-reimagine-the-supercenter) — produce sight lines, front services, electronics, automotive, checkout, and baby-department examples.
- [171 Prototype Supercenter plan index](https://www.agcetnplanroom.com/projects/2764/plans/new-walmart-171-proto-supercenter-7239-building-only-22896) — current 170,000-square-foot plan package and its distinct building, grocery, service, specialist, fulfillment, and fixture systems.
- [RetailStat 2025 Supercenter Format](https://rs-api.retailstat.com/uploads/Copy_of_Supercenter_Format_2025_Abridged_1_a621c06f09.pdf) — format scale and the balance among grocery sales, general merchandise, backroom work, and fulfillment.
- [Walmart Store Maps](https://www.walmart.com/cp/find-an-item-store-maps/6422847) — evidence that department/aisle legibility is an operating feature, not just decoration.

These references are used to identify retail grammar. Big Retail should develop
its own brands, visual identity, proportions, and player-created layouts rather
than reproducing a specific Walmart store.
