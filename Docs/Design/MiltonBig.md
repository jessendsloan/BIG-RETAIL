# Big Retail — Milton "Mr. BIG" Big

**Status:** Accepted character foundation; campaign arc intentionally broad

## Identity

**Milton "Mr. BIG" Big** is the founder and owner of **BIG**, a sprawling private business empire whose interests include **BIG Wholesale**, **BIG Finance**, and potentially other BIG companies encountered later.

Almost nobody calls him Milton. Employees, vendors, bankers, and business associates know him as **Mr. BIG**.

The player begins Big Retail already entangled with his ecosystem:

- BIG helped finance the player's beginning.
- Mr. BIG owns or controls the player's startup debt through BIG Finance.
- BIG Wholesale is the player's easiest opening source of merchandise.
- Mr. BIG therefore has a legitimate reason to remain involved in the player's growth.

He is not merely a supplier character.

He is the primary recurring human spine of the campaign.

---

## Core role

Mr. BIG occupies three roles at the same time:

### Tutor

He knows retail, finance, distribution, property, and scale far better than the player does at the beginning.

His tutorial function should usually arrive through the fiction of business rather than as a detached game tutorial.

**Core writing rule:**

> Mr. BIG usually teaches because he is trying to sell, finance, provide, or recommend something.

He should rarely say:

> "You have to do this because the tutorial says so."

His natural mode is closer to:

> "Have I got something for you."

The mechanic and the story should therefore reinforce each other.

### Safety Net

BIG sells convenience.

Mr. BIG should remain genuinely useful rather than becoming a fake beginner trap that the player permanently outgrows.

BIG Wholesale is broad, fast, dependable, and forgiving. When the player forgets an order, needs emergency inventory, wants a small quantity, or has not yet built a more efficient supply arrangement, BIG can solve the problem.

The player should sometimes knowingly pay too much because BIG is the correct option for the current situation.

### Commercial Opponent

Mr. BIG is not necessarily the villain.

He is the player's **commercial opponent**.

Every deal is real. Every service is useful. The terms are simply tilted toward BIG whenever Milton has the leverage to do so.

He likes leverage, especially his own.

The player should gradually learn when Milton's offer is worth accepting and when they can create a better alternative.

---

## Character

Mr. BIG should be:

- enormously confident
- charming
- commercially ruthless
- surprisingly knowledgeable
- paternal without becoming sentimental
- funny
- slightly unfair
- almost never stupid
- rarely angry
- delighted by business itself

He genuinely likes the player.

He may become genuinely proud of the player.

Neither feeling prevents him from charging the player heavily for convenience.

Those ideas are completely compatible in Milton's mind.

### Character principle

Mr. BIG does not need to threaten the player.

His power comes from ownership, capital, access, information, and favorable positioning.

The emotional contrast is important:

> He can be warm while the deal is bad for you.

The intended recurring reaction is approximately:

> **"Oh, this bastard. ...Okay, what've you got?"**

---

## Visual canon

The approved character portrait establishes the canonical visual source for Milton Big.

The portrait represents **Mr. BIG around the moment he crossed his first billion dollars**.

Future depictions should derive from that image rather than reinventing him.

Key traits:

- huge, physically imposing build
- bald / closely shaven head
- thick neck and broad shoulders
- immaculate expensive suit and vest
- gold accessories
- cigar as a signature prop
- relaxed physical dominance rather than aggression
- warm, confident, slightly predatory smile

The visual inspiration is the archetype of an imposing suit-wearing business magnate, but Milton should remain his own character: more jovial, more salesman-like, more paternal, and commercially predatory rather than physically threatening.

---

## Voice direction

Do **not** assume full campaign voice-over.

Preferred direction:

- written dialogue carries most content
- Mr. BIG has a reusable library of voiced tags, reactions, laughs, greetings, approvals, and sales-pitch fragments
- rare major campaign moments may receive a fully voiced line

Examples of reusable vocal assets:

- greeting
- laugh
- amused approval
- disappointment
- "Kid!"
- "That's business."
- "BIG can handle it."
- "Now you're thinking."

The goal is for the player to recognize **the sound of Milton Big** without making dialogue rewrites dependent on thousands of recorded lines.

# Campaign function

## Narrative-system rule

Whenever possible, Mr. BIG should represent forces that already exist inside the simulation.

He should not function as a generic quest dispenser.

His narrative appearances should introduce, pressure, explain, or reframe real systems such as:

- startup capital
- debt
- purchasing
- BIG Wholesale
- expansion financing
- property acquisition
- commercial leverage
- supplier competition
- economies of scale

This is the desired Anno-like structure: narrative gives human context to systems, while playing the systems advances the player's understanding of the narrative relationship.

---

## Opening campaign spine

Keep this broad until the opening gameplay flow is implemented.

### Beat 1 — The Opportunity

Mr. BIG gives the player a shot at becoming a retailer.

He provides or arranges startup financing and helps establish the player's control of the initial corner-store property.

The player's first impression should be that Milton is extremely useful and perhaps even reassuring.

### Beat 2 — Make It a Store

The player uses that opportunity to establish the minimum functioning store.

Milton can frame the objective, but he should not narrate every construction click.

### Beat 3 — Shelves Don't Sell Air

The physical store exists, but it needs merchandise.

Milton naturally introduces **BIG Wholesale** as the easiest solution.

The player learns Products / Purchasing through an offer that makes sense in-world.

### Beat 4 — First Purchase Order

The player buys opening inventory through BIG Wholesale.

At this point BIG is deliberately the obvious answer: broad selection, immediate access, fast service, no serious planning requirement.

### Beat 5 — Physical Delivery

A BIG vehicle arrives and merchandise enters the store through Receiving.

The simulation itself should provide much of the payoff here.

### Beat 6 — Stock and Sell

The player gets merchandise onto fixtures, opens the store, and makes the first sales.

The complete retail chain becomes visible:

**Milton's capital → store → supplier → order → receiving → fixture → customer → revenue**

### Beat 7 — The First Crack

Once the player understands the basic retail loop, the cost of dependence becomes visible.

A cheaper independent supplier, an approaching debt payment, or another economic comparison demonstrates that BIG's convenience has a price.

This should be a discovery, not a villain reveal.

Milton has not lied. The player is simply becoming capable of understanding the deal.

### Beat 8 — First Expansion Handoff

Mr. BIG also tutorials the **first land-region purchase**.

After this first handoff, permanent land acquisition should become player-directed rather than requiring Milton for every expansion.

# Long relationship arc

## Phase I — Patron

Mr. BIG knows more than the player and controls resources the player needs.

He has money, products, contacts, infrastructure, and experience.

The player is dependent on him.

## Phase II — Useful Exploiter

The player begins discovering alternatives and understands that BIG's convenience carries a premium.

Milton should not become offended when the player comparison-shops.

He respects competent business behavior.

## Phase III — One Option Among Many

The player develops independent suppliers, stronger infrastructure, better planning, and greater financial capacity.

BIG remains useful, but dependence becomes strategic rather than automatic.

Milton increasingly recognizes the player as a serious operator.

## Phase IV — Competitor / Counterparty

The player's growing retail power begins intersecting with BIG's interests.

The exact form is not yet locked. Possibilities may involve wholesale business, financing, property, supplier relationships, or another BIG subsidiary.

The important change is that Milton can no longer assume that he sets every term.

## Phase V — Peer

At mega-retail scale, the relationship reverses.

Mr. BIG may want the player's purchasing volume, business, partnership, financing relationship, or commercial cooperation.

The man who once said:

> "Here's what you're gonna do, kid."

must eventually hear what **the player** is proposing.

The intended emotional payoff is not defeating an evil businessman.

It is becoming good enough at business that Milton can no longer simply rake the player.

---

# Campaign climax — open design question

The campaign probably needs a real climax before releasing the player into the continuing sandbox.

**Do not lock the exact plot yet.**

The climax should pay off the central Milton/player relationship rather than introduce an unrelated final threat.

Preferred structural principle:

> **The climax should force the player to use the retail institution they built to negotiate, resist, outgrow, or redefine a major deal with Mr. BIG.**

The final challenge should therefore be both:

- a narrative payoff to the patron / opponent / peer arc, and
- a systems payoff requiring the store to function at the scale the campaign has taught the player to build.

Potential directions to explore later include:

- Milton proposes a major deal that would place the player back under BIG's control, and the player must become strong enough to reject or renegotiate it.
- BIG and the player's retailer become direct commercial counterparties for a major supply / property / financing arrangement.
- A final debt or ownership issue creates a concrete independence test.
- Milton himself deliberately tests whether the player has become capable of sitting across the table as an equal.

The desired final relationship is more interesting than simple victory over a villain:

> **The player earns leverage. Milton recognizes it. The campaign ends with the relationship fundamentally changed.**

After that climax, the same store continues into sandbox play.

---

## Guardrails

- Do not turn Milton into a moustache-twirling capitalist villain.
- Do not make every external system secretly owned by BIG; recurrence is fun, total ownership makes the world feel fake.
- Do not make him a constant quest-giver.
- Do not make BIG Wholesale intentionally useless just to prove Milton is exploitative.
- Do not resolve his entire arc before the gameplay systems that carry it exist.
- Preserve humor, but let Milton retain weight, competence, and real commercial power.

Use this document as the character authority while designing the opening campaign flow.

For now, story work should stay at the **beat / intent level** until the relevant gameplay systems are playable.

The immediate narrative design question is:
